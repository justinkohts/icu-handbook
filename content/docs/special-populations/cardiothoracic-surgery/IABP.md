## Overview
An intra-aortic balloon pump (IABP) provides temporary circulatory support by **counterpulsation**. A helium-filled balloon in the descending thoracic aorta inflates during diastole and deflates immediately before systole.

- **Diastolic inflation:** Increases aortic diastolic pressure and may improve coronary perfusion.
- **Presystolic deflation:** Reduces LV afterload, wall stress, and myocardial oxygen demand.
- **Hemodynamic effect:** Modest increase in cardiac output (~0.5–1 L/min), dependent on native cardiac function.

![IABP mechanism](/images/special-populations/cardiothoracic-surgery/iabp_mechanism.png)



## Indications
Like all mechanical circulatory support devices, IABP is used as a bridge to recovery or definitive treatment.

- **Mechanical complications of MI:** acute severe MR, post-MI VSD.
- **Cardiac surgery:** difficulty separating from bypass, or low-output state after cardiac surgery.
- Selected high risk MI and LV failure.

> ⚠️ Not recommended routinely in acute MI related cardiogenic shock.


## Contraindications / Cautions
- **Cardiac disease:**
  - Severe aortic regurgitation.
  - LV outflow tract obstruction.
  - Chronic end stage cardiomyopathy (no recovery/reversibility).
- **Aortic/vascular disease:**
  - Aortic dissection.
  - Significant aortic aneurysm.
  - Recent aortic surgery (including prosthetic aortic grafts).
  - Severe peripheral artery disease.
- **Procedural risk:**
  - Severe coagulopathy or active bleeding.
  - Access site infection.
- **No exit strategy:** no viable recovery or definitive treatment pathway. 


## Placement and Initiation
### Insertion & Position
The cardiothoracic team or a cardiologist inserts the IABP usually through the femoral artery, with fluoroscopy or TOE guidance.

The tip should lie just distal to left subclavian artery origin. On CXR, it is [~2 cm above the carina](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17717232/).

Malposition can result in ischemia. Too proximal risks arm/cerebral ischemia and aortic injury. Too distal risks renal/mesenteric ischemia and ineffective counterpulsation.


### Initial Settings
- **Trigger:** ECG trigger (timed to R wave) is preferred, usually via lead II. Arterial pressure trigger as backup. If paced, pacing spikes can be used.
- **Assist ratio/frequency:** 1:1, i.e. every beat is assisted.
- **Augmentation:** 100% (max).


### Monitoring
Monitoring focuses on hemodynamic support, end-organ perfusion, and vascular complications.

- **Hemodynamics:** MAP, arterial waveform, augmentation, CO, vasoactive requirements.
- **Perfusion:** Lactate, urine output, peripheral perfusion.
- **Vascular:** Distal pulses, capillary refill, access site bleeding.
- **Device:** Triggering, balloon timing, assist ratio, alarms, catheter position.


### Anticoagulation
Anticoagulation with IV unfractionated heparin is recommended after 48 hours of IABP in situ. Patient undergoing prolonged weaning with less than 1:1 support should be considered for anticoagulation.

If the patient has been anti-coagulated then the heparin should be discontinued for 3 hours prior to removal of the IABP. 


## Waveforms
### Normal Waveform with Optimal Performance
- Inflation begins at the **dicrotic notch**.
- **Augmented diastolic pressure** is higher than unassisted systolic pressure.
- **Assisted end-diastolic pressure** is lower than unassisted end-diastolic pressure by 15 mmHg.
- **Assisted systolic pressure** is lower than unassisted systolic pressure by 5 mmHg.

![IABP normal](/images/special-populations/cardiothoracic-surgery/iabp_normal.jpg)


## Weaning
Weaning involves reducing the IABP support in a stepwise fashion. It should be considered when the patient's condition has stabilized.

Broadly speaking, weaning involves:
- Decreasing the **assist ratio** 1:1 → 1:2 → 1:3. Decreasing augmentation is less commonly used.
- Monitoring cardiac function, MAP, and end-organ perfusion after weaning.
- Remove IABP if reduced support is tolerated.

> ⚠️ Avoid prolonged pump interruption or low-frequency assistance, particularly without anticoagulation, because of the risk of balloon thrombosis.


## Removal
Removal is a vascular procedure as the large arterial hole needs planned hemostasis. It is performed by the team who inserted the IABP. Hemostasis may involve manual pressure for 30 minutes, a femoral compression system, or a vascular closure device.

If the patient has been anti-coagulated then the heparin should be discontinued for 3 hours prior to removal of the IABP. 

After removal, distal pulse checks and flat bed rest are required.


## Troubleshooting
### Waveform Abnormalities
Waveform abnormalities can be caused by **inappropriate timing**. Correct balloon timing can be assessed by comparing assisted and unassisted beats, typically using a **temporary** 1:2 assist ratio. The timing can then be adjusted to ensure optimal support.

![IABP abnormal](/images/special-populations/cardiothoracic-surgery/iabp_abnormal.png)


### Pump Stopping / Loss of Augmentation
**Insufficient support** could be caused by device malfunction, inappropriate timing, balloon perforation, or a change in patient's hemodynamic status.

- Assess hemodynamics and clinical perfusion.
- Check triggering, ECG/arterial signal, helium supply, connections, power, and alarms.
- Assess for arrhythmias, catheter displacement, or balloon malfunction.
- Escalate urgently if support cannot be restored. 
- Optimize hemodynamic support with medical therapies, and arrange for pump replacement if required.

> ⚠️ Prolonged interruption increases the risk of balloon thrombosis.

### Blood in gas tubing
Blood in the helium tubing suggests **balloon rupture**, risking helium embolism, balloon thrombosis, and entrapment. See this [case report](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42130582/) for an example image of blood in gas tubing.

- Stop balloon cycling immediately.
- Clamp/isolate gas tubing.
- Arrange urgent removal.
- Do not restart inflation or forcibly remove a resistant balloon.

## Complications
- **Vascular:** limb ischemia, hemorrhage (including retroperitoneal), aortic or femoral dissection.
- **Malposition:** arch/vessel occlusion (left arm, stroke), renal or mesenteric occlusion.
- **Hematologic:** thrombocytopenia, hemolysis.
- **Device:** balloon rupture with gas embolism.
- **Infection.**
