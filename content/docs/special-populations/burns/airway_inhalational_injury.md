## Airway Management in Burns
Suspect airway injury in patients with the following:
- **Mechanism:** enclosed-space fire, prolonged smoke exposure, explosion, hot gas/steam exposure.
- **Symptoms:** sore throat, dysphagia, voice changes, hoarseness, cough, dyspnea.
- **Examination:** intraoral burns, oropharyngeal soot, erythema, edema, deep facial/neck burns, carbonaceous sputum.
- **High-risk findings:** stridor, progressive facial/oropharyngeal edema, respiratory distress, impaired airway protection (reduced GCS).

Consider **early intubation** for:
- Established or progressive upper-airway obstruction.
- Deep intraoral burns or significant oropharyngeal edema.
- Respiratory failure, respiratory fatigue, inability to protect the airway.
- Extensive deep facial/neck burns or major burns with anticipated progressive edema.
- Major burns requiring prolonged transfer where airway deterioration may occur without appropriate airway expertise.

> ⚠️ These cases should be considered a **potentially difficult intubation**. Prepare for difficult intubation, consult senior support (Duty Intensivist, Anesthetist) early.


## Specific Airway Management Considerations
- **Progressive edema:** airway swelling may worsen over the first 24–48 hours, especially during fluid resuscitation.
- **Difficult airway:** anticipate a difficult airway due to distorted anatomy, limited mouth opening, edema, and difficulty with bag-mask ventilation. Involve an experienced airway-trained clinician early.
- **Neuromuscular blockade:** suxamethonium may be used within the first 24 hours if otherwise appropriate. Avoid after 24 hours due to the risk of severe hyperkalemia.
- **ETT fixation:** consider dental wiring in facial burns, which may prevent conventional adhesive fixation.
- **Bronchoscopy:** useful after airway stabilization to identify and grade lower airway injury and clear secretions or casts.
- **Circumferential chest burns:** monitor chest expansion and ventilation. Consider escharotomy if the eschar restricts ventilation.


## Inhalational Injury
Inhalational injury can be divided into 3 overlapping components:

| Type | Mechanism | Clinical features |
|---|---|---|
| **Upper airway thermal injury** | Direct thermal injury mainly to supraglottic structures, causing inflammation and progressive edema | Hoarseness, dysphagia, stridor, airway obstruction |
| **Lower airway inhalation injury** | Chemical and particulate injury causing epithelial damage, bronchospasm, mucus production, and airway casts | Cough, wheeze, carbonaceous sputum, hypoxemia |
| **Systemic toxicity** | CO reduces oxygen transport and utilization; cyanide inhibits mitochondrial respiration | Altered consciousness, lactic acidosis, cardiovascular collapse |

- Administer 100% O₂ when significant smoke inhalation or CO toxicity is suspected.
- Obtain blood gas (for gas exchange, COHb, lactate).
- CO toxicity can be measured using blood gas analysis.
- Consider cyanide toxicity in severe enclosed-space smoke exposure, with cardiovascular collapse or marked lactic acidosis.
- Children may have prolonged smoke and chemical exposure, as they often hide rather than escape a fire.
