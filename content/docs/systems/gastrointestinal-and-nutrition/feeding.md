## Overview
Critical illness produces a hypermetabolic and catabolic response, characterized by insulin resistance, increased protein breakdown, and skeletal muscle loss. **Nutritional support** aims to minimize nutritional deficits, preserve lean body mass, and support recovery.

**Early enteral nutrition (EN) is preferred**, provided the patient is adequately resuscitated and has sufficient gastrointestinal function. However, **early full-calorie feeding should be avoided**, with nutritional delivery increased progressively as the patient stabilizes.


## Nutritional Assessment and Targets
Perform **nutritional assessment** within 24–48 hours of ICU admission, with dietitian involvement and regular reassessment. Consider patients requiring more than 48 hours of ICU care at risk of malnutrition.

### Energy Requirements
Energy requirements vary with the severity and phase of critical illness. Indirect calorimetry is unavailable at Waikato Hospital, so a weight-based estimate is used to guide nutritional delivery.

- **Estimated energy requirements:** 20–25 kcal/kg/day (use Adjusted Body Weight for obese patients).
- Deliver calories incrementally:
  - **Early acute phase (days 1–3):** Hypocaloric feeding, ≤ 70% of estimated requirements.
  - **After day 3:** Progressively advance calorie delivery according to clinical recovery.
> ⚠️ Account for nonnutritional calories, including propofol and IV dextrose. An infusion of Propofol 1% at 20 mL/h yields ~530 kcal per day, almost equivalent to a Big Mac!

### Protein Requirements
Critical illness accelerates skeletal muscle proteolysis, while inflammation and anabolic resistance limit the ability of protein supplementation to prevent muscle wasting.

- **Protein target:** 1.3 g/kg/day (deliver progressively).
- Higher protein delivery has not consistently improved outcomes and may be harmful in patients with AKI.
- Protein requirements should be reassessed during recovery and rehabilitation.


## Delivery of Nutrition
### Enteral Nutrition (EN)
Initiate EN within 24–48 hours when oral intake is inadequate and the patient is sufficiently stabilized. Gastric feeding is the preferred initial route, starting at a low rate and advancing according to tolerance.

EN can generally continue during mechanical ventilation, prone positioning, ECMO, and stable vasopressor support. However, feeding should be delayed in uncontrolled shock, suspected bowel ischemia, or mechanical bowel obstruction.

#### Feed Intolerance
- Assess for vomiting, abdominal distension, and gastrointestinal dysfunction.
- Consider prokinetics (metoclopramide or erythromycin).
- Consider post-pyloric feeding (via NJ tube) for persistent gastric intolerance or high aspiration risk.

#### Aspiration Prevention and Feeding Safety
- Maintain head-of-bed elevation 30–45°, unless contraindicated.
- Confirm NG tube placement before initial feeding using CXR.
- Reassess NG tube placement following suspected displacement.


### Parenteral Nutrition
Parenteral nutrition (PN) provides nutrients intravenously, usually via CVC or PICC. It is used when enteral nutrition is contraindicated or persistently inadequate to meet nutritional goals.

- Consider PN **within 3–7 days** when oral and enteral feeding are contraindicated.
- **Earlier progressive PN may be appropriate** in severely malnourished patients.
- Avoid routine early supplemental PN solely to achieve full caloric targets during the first week.

Complications include catheter-related infections, hyperglycemia, hypertriglyceridemia, electrolyte disturbances, and hepatic dysfunction.

## Monitoring and Complications
Nutrition delivery and tolerance should be regularly reassessed, with particular attention to:
- **Nutritional goals vs delivery:** Actual calorie and protein delivery compared with prescribed targets, including nonnutritional calories.
- **Feed (in)tolerance:** Vomiting, abdominal distension, diarrhea, constipation, signs of aspiration.
- **Metabolic complications:** Blood glucose, phosphate, potassium, magnesium, fluid balance.
- **Parenteral nutrition:** Monitor triglycerides, liver function, catheter-related complications.

### Refeeding Syndrome
Refeeding syndrome is a potentially **life-threatening metabolic complication** following the reintroduction of nutrition in malnourished patients. Carbohydrate intake stimulates insulin secretion, causing intracellular shifts of phosphate, potassium, and magnesium. This results in **hypophosphatemia, hypokalemia, and hypomagnesemia**. Increased thiamine requirements and fluid retention further contribute to metabolic disturbances. 

**Severe cases** may cause arrhythmias, cardiac dysfunction, respiratory muscle weakness, and neurological dysfunction.

**Risk factors:**
- Prolonged starvation.
- Significant weight loss.
- Low BMI.
- Chronic alcohol use disorder.
- Pre-existing electrolyte deficiencies.

**Prevention and management:**
- Administer thiamine before initating feeding.
- Correct electrolyte deficiencies.
- Gradual cautious intiation and progression of nutrition (begin at ~10–20 kcal/kg/day in at-risk patients).
- Monitor phosphate, potassium, and magnesium regularly (q12h for the first 72 hours in high-risk patients).
- If refeeding syndrome develops, replace electrolytes and temporarily reduce caloric delivery until stabilized.
