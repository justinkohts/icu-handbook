## Overview
Ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) is pneumonia developing more than 48 hours after endotracheal intubation. It is a common ICU-acquired infection associated with prolonged mechanical ventilation, increased ICU length of stay, and increased mortality.

VAP typically results from microaspiration of colonized oropharyngeal secretions around the endotracheal tube cuff, with impaired airway clearance and bacterial biofilm formation contributing to infection.



## Diagnosis
Diagnosis remains challenging due to the lack of a universally accepted diagnostic standard and difficulty distinguishing infection from airway colonization.

VAP is a clinical diagnosis supported by respiratory microbiology. No single clinical, radiological, or microbiological finding reliably confirms VAP.

Suspect VAP in mechanically ventilated patients developing respiratory or systemic issues:
- Respiratory: New or progressive pulmonary infiltrates, purulent secretions, worsening oxygenation, or increasing ventilatory requirements.
- Systemic: Fever or hypothermia, leukocytosis or leukopenia, hemodynamic instability.

Investigations:
- Endotracheal aspirate: Preferred initial respiratory sample for Gram stain and semiquantitative culture.
- Bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL): Consider when the diagnosis is uncertain, alternative pathology is suspected, or additional sampling is required.
- Blood cultures: Obtain before antibiotics where feasible.
- Chest imaging: Evaluate for new or progressive infiltrates and alternative diagnoses.

Interpret respiratory cultures cautiously, as airway colonization is common. Consider alternative causes of deterioration, including atelectasis, pulmonary edema, ARDS, and pulmonary embolism.

Obtain microbiological samples before antimicrobial therapy where feasible, without delaying treatment in critically ill patients.
