## Overview

Liberation from mechanical ventilation should be considered as soon as the underlying reason for ventilation begins to improve. Remember that invasive mechanical ventilation [is not benign]({{% relref "invasive-mechanical-ventilation#risks-and-complications" %}}).

{{< concept-card title="General Approach" >}}
- **Vent weaning readiness:** SBT, low vent settings, improved underlying pathology.
- **Extubation readiness:** airway protection, cough, secretion burden.
- **Extubation**.
- **Post-extubation respiratory support:** nasal cannulae, Hudson mask, HFNO, NIV.
- **Exit/rescue strategy:** reintubation, NIV, HFNO, limit ceiling of treatment.
{{< /concept-card >}}


## Assess Readiness for Ventilation Weaning
Generally, the patient should have:
- Underlying cause for respiratory failure improving.
- Adequate oxygenation (FiO₂ 0.4–0.5) with acceptable SpO₂.
- Low/moderate PEEP (≤ 5–8 cmH₂O).
- Ability to initiate spontaneous breaths.
- Hemodynamic stability (low dose vasoactive drugs).
- Adequate level of consciousness with minimal sedation.
- No major metabolic, electrolyte, temperature, or acid-base disturbance.


### Spontaneous Breathing Trial (SBT)
The SBT is the primary test of whether a patient can **sustain** spontaneous ventilation. It has a high positive predictive value for successful extubation (80–90%).

There are multiple common techniques:
- **Pressure support trial:** 5–8 cmH₂O over PEEP 5 cmH₂O.
- **CPAP trial:** 5–8 cmH₂O, with no pressure support, ± automatic tube compensation.
- **T-piece trial:** no ventilator assistance.

In general, a trial lasts 30–120 minutes. Most patients who fail, do so relatively early. **SBT failure is a clinical diagnosis**; an SBT should be stopped if they become progressively clinically unstable.


#### SBT outcome
There is no single criteria used to judge SBT success or failure. A combination of parameters are usually used.
 
| **Feature** | **SBT success** | **SBT failure** |
|---|---|---|
| **Clinical appearance** | Comfortable, no distress | Dyspnea, distress, agitation, diaphoresis |
| **Work of breathing** | Stable, minimal accessory muscle use | ↑ accessory muscle use, paradoxical breathing |
| **Respiratory rate** | < 30–35/min and stable | > 30–35/min persistently or progressively ↑ |
| **Oxygenation** | SpO₂ stable | New/worsening hypoxemia or ↑ O₂ requirement |
| **Ventilation** | Stable ventilation and pH | Progressive hypercapnia or respiratory acidosis |
| **Heart rate/rhythm** | Stable | Marked tachycardia, bradycardia, or new significant arrhythmia |
| **Blood pressure** | Stable | Significant hypertension or hypotension |
| **Neurologic status** | Alert or unchanged | Agitation, anxiety, ↓ consciousness |


SBT tests ventilatory reserve / work of breathing. It does not fully test airway protection, secretion clearance, cough strength, upper airway patency, or neurological function. Therefore, the decision to extubate should integrate all these factors.


### Rapid Shallow Breathing Index (RSBI)

$$
RSBI = \frac{Respiratory\ rate}{Tidal\ volume\ (in\ liters)}
$$

An **RSBI <105** was historically used to predict successful liberation from ventilation. However, its disciminatory power is limited even at lower cutoff values, with moderate sensitivity and poor specificity. The RSBI is primarily designed to predict **weaning readiness** and does not assess airway protection, cough strength, secretion clearance, or neurological function. As such, it [should not be used as a standalone predictor of extubation success](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35680324/).

The RSBI should also not be used to determine readiness for an SBT, as it [prolongs time on the ventilator](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16878032/).



## Assess Readiness for Extubation
After passing the SBT, assess the following.
- **Airway protection and patency:**
  - **Lower GCS** and **inability to follow commands** are associated with higher extubation failure ([particularly in neurocritical care patients](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35197209/)), as they may be less able to maintain their own upper airway patency.
  - Patients at significant risk of **post-extubation laryngeal edema** (traumatic/difficult intubation, prolonged intubation, laryngeal inflammation) may benefit from a **cuff leak test**.
- **Cough:**
  - A strong suction-induced or voluntary cough is reassuring, as it predicts the patient's ability to clear secretions.
  - Cough peak flow through the ETT may also be used to quantify cough assessment.
- **Secretion burden:**
  - Assess the secretion volume, consistency, and frequency of suctioning.
  - A high secretion burden is more concerning for extubation failure.


#### Risk factors for extubation failure
- **Reduced reserve:** older age, COPD/chronic lung disease, heart failure, anemia.
- **Difficulty weaning:** prolonged ventilation, poor oxygenation, previous failed SBTs, severe critical illness.
- **Airway incompetence:** ↓ consciousness, weak cough, excessive secretions.

A **tracheostomy** may be indicated in patients who are at high risk of extubation failure.


## Post-Extubation Respiratory Support
Before extubation, decide post-extubation respiratory support as well as an exit strategy (*e.g. would/should this patient be reintubated if they worsen?*).

Broadly speaking, the strategies are:
- **Lower-risk patient:** extubate onto conventional O₂ therapy or HFNO.
- **Higher-risk patient:** extubate onto HFNO or NIV.
