## Overview
Prone positioning is a mortality-reducing therapy for moderate-to-severe ARDS, when started early and combined with lung-protective ventilation.


## Indications
- ARDS with PaO₂/FiO₂ <150 mmHg despite optimized ventilation and PEEP.


## Cautions and Contraindications
- **Absolute contraindications:**
  - Unstable spinal fracture.
  - Open chest, < 24 hours after cardiac surgery.
  - Central cannulation for VA-ECMO or BiVAD support.
- **Relative contraindications:**
  - **Severe hemodynamic instability.**
  - **Raised intracranial pressure.**
  - **Unstable ortho injuries:** pelvic fracture, long bone fractures, spinal cord injury.
  - **Surgical:** recent abdominal/thoracic surgery, open abdomen.
  - **Staff:** untrained/insufficient staff, equipment constraints.


## Mechanism
Proning produces more homogenous lung ventilation and stress:
- **Recruits dorsal lung** and reduces dependent atelectasis.
- **Reduces ventral lung overdistension** and regional stress/strain.
- **Improves V/Q matching** and reduces shunt.
- **Reduces atelectrauma** (cyclic alveolar opening/closure).
- May reduce pulmonary vascular resistance and right ventricular strain. 


## Procedure
Watch [this video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JNb4owwdx-Y) on the steps of proning a ventilated patient.

### Proning
Broadly speaking, the steps are:
- **Before turning:**
  - Use a checklist.
  - Use a trained team, with a designated leader (~6 staff).
  - Pre-oxygenate, optimize sedation and paralysis.
  - Secure ETT, lines, drains, tubes, urinary catheter.
  - Prepare emergency airway equipment.
  - Prepare pillows and positioning supports.
- **Turning:**
  - Wrap patient tightly in a sheet.
  - Slide patient toward one end of the bed (usually away from ventilator).
  - Turn the patient lateral (usually facing the ventilator).
  - Turn the patient prone (head usually facing the ventilator).
- **After turning:**
  - Reconfirm ETT depth, capnography, ventilation, vital signs.
  - Position head safely and ensure no pressure areas on airway and eyes.
  - For arms and head, use a **Swimmer's Position** (with patient's hand up on the same side as the patient's face).
  - Check other pressure areas - protect eyes, face, ears, breasts, genitalia, pelvis, bony prominences. Allow the abdomen to hang relatively free.
  - Check all lines and drains. Reconnect monitoring which may have been removed.

The patient should **remain prone usually for ≥ 16 consecutive hours**. To reduce the complications associated with prone positioning, the patient should be de-proned/supinated regularly. There is no strong evidence keeping all patients continuously prone for 24–48+ hours.

### De-proning / Supinating
Similar to proning, use a checklist, pre-oxygenate, ensure tubes/lines are secure. Wrap the patient, move away from the ventilator, so that when turning, the patient will be facing the ventilator. Ideally, this should be done in the morning due to increased ICU and allied healthcare professional staffing.


## Risks and Complications
- ETT obstruction, kinking, displacement.
- Pressure injury, facial edema, peripheral nerve injury (e.g. brachial plexus), ocular injury.
- Dislodgement/kinking of vascular lines, drains.
- Restricted airway and vascular access during emergencies.


## CPR and Defibrillation while Prone
If cardiac arrest occurs while prone, CPR may be started prone while preparing for safe supination. For an intubated patient, deliver **compressions over approximately T7–T9** (i.e. slightly inferior to the angle of scapula) until the patient can be turned safely. While performing CPR prone, counterpressure under the sternum with an additional staff member's hand or a 1 liter IV fluid bag may be used. 

**Defibrillator pads** may be placed in the postero-lateral or bi-axillary positions.
