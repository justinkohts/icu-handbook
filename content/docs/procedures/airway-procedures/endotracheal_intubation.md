## Overview
Endotracheal intubation is a high risk but life-saving procedure often performed in non-ideal circumstances with patients who are haemodynamically unstable and limited physiological reserve.

⚠️ Always call for assistance, which may include the Duty Anaesthetist or more experienced colleagues.


## Indications
- Airway protection 
  - Loss of protective reflexes (e.g. reduced consciousness)
  - Upper airway obstruction, threat or impending threat
- Improving gas exchange
  - Inadequate oxygenation
  - Inadequate ventilation
  - Control of arterial CO2 content (e.g. traumatic brain injury)
- Facilitate safe patient transportation
- Tracheal toileting - suction of respiratory secretions
- Significant aspiration risk - e.g. upper GI bleed


## Suggested Workflow for Planned Intubation
⚠️ First and foremost, **utilize a checklist!** The SOAPME mnemonic may also be useful.

{{< concept-card title="Mnemonic: SOAPME" >}}
- **Suction**
  - At least 1 working suction, turned on and placed between mattress and bed
- **Oxygen**
  - Non-rebreathe mask and ambu bag attached to 15 L/min O2 + turned on
  - Nasal prongs or HFNO apneic oxygenation
- **Airways**
  - **ETT:** #7.5 ETT with stylet fits most adults, #7.0 for smaller females, #8.0 for larger males. Test cuff by filling with 10 mL of air into pilot balloon.
  - **Stylet:** if used, place inside ETT for rigidity. Bed at 30° at the level of cuff. Ensure the stylet tip stops 0.5–1 cm before end of tube.
  - **Laryngoscope blade:** Mac 3 or 4 for adults.
  - **Laryngoscope handle:** attach blade and ensure light source works.
  - **Other airways & adjuncts:** video laryngoscope, LMA, bougie, surgical cric kit. 
  - ⚠️ Ensure you have **all** of these at the bedside!
- **Pre-oxygenate**
  - 15 L/min non-rebreathe mask or HFNO
- **Monitoring / Medications**
  - ECG, BP cuff (on opposite arm to IV), pulse oximetry
  - Medications drawn up and ready to be given
- **End tidal CO2**

{{% steps %}}

### Team
- Are the right **number** of people present with the **appropriate skill mix**?
- e.g. 1st intubator, 2nd intubator, airway assistant, drugs, team leader, ACNM.
- **Is additional support needed?** e.g. duty anesthetist, pediatric anesthetist.
- See [team roles](#team-roles) for more detail.

### Drugs
- **Dedicated person** to induce anesthetic and give muscle relaxant.
- Are the **drugs and doses** appropriate for the patient's physiological state?
- Consider **vasopressors** and 'push dose' adrenaline.
- Draw up post-intubation infusions.

### Assessment of Airway
- See [pre-intubation assessment](#pre-intubation-assessment)
- Involves **prediction** of difficult bag mask ventilation, laryngoscopy, and tracheal intubation.
- **Recognition of a physiologically difficult airway** is also paramount.
- **Optimize first-pass success:** bougie/stylet, video laryngoscopy, patient positioning.
- **Verbalize intubation plan** during checklist.
- For a [physiologically difficult airway](#recognition-of-physiologically-difficult-airway), priorities include:
  - Resuscitation before intubation
  - Optimizing oxygenation
  - Optimizing circulation
- **Considerations:**
  - How quickly will the patient desaturate?
  - After sympatholysis, is there a likelihood of physiological compromise?
  - Will the cardiovascular system tolerate positive pressure ventilation? (e.g. severe COPD, massive PE, RV dysfunction)
  - Are there any special considerations? (TBI, raised ICP)
  - Is there a significant aspiration risk? (UGIB, pregnancy, obesity)
  - Do I need an arterial line prior to intubation?
  
  
⚠️ Be aware of **tachypnea in the context of metabolic acidosis** e.g. DKA or salicylate poisoning. Removal of the respiratory drive by intubation will likely result in post-induction arrest.
  

### Patient preparation
- **Optimize position:**
  - Align the ear-sternal notch line.
  - Consider ramping the patient. Do I need more towels/pillows or the ramping device (TROOP pillow) from theaters?
- **Pre-oxygenate:**
  - Pre-oxygenate all patients before induction of general anaesthesia.
  - Pre-oxygenation should be performed in the head-up position and with a technique allowing positive pressure, where feasible.
  - Especially in patients with a risk of difficult airway management, pre-oxygenate with high-flow nasal oxygen or CPAP/NIV. 

### Checklist
- **Verbalize checklist** and ensure patient, staff, and equipment are prepared.

### Intubation & confirmation of EtCO2
- Confirm ETT placement with **sustained exhaled CO2**:
  - Level rises with exhalation, falls with inspiration.
  - Consistent or increasing in amplitude over at least 7 breaths.
  - Peak amplitude >1 kPa (7.5 mmHg) above baseline.
  - Clinically appropriate.

### Post-Intubation
- Connect to ventilator.
- Start infusions: sedatives, vasoactive drugs.

## Pre-Intubation Assessment
Intubation can be difficult due to **anatomical** or **physiological** challenges. Assessing a difficult airway involves identifying anatomical features or clinical conditions that may compromise oxygenation during mask ventilation or ETT placement.

#### Prediction of difficult bag mask ventilation
- The mnemonic MOANS or BONES may help predict difficult bag mask ventilation.
- **MOANS:** **M**ask seal issues, **O**besity/Obstruction, **A**ge >55, **N**o teeth, **S**leep apnea or **S**tiff lungs.
- **BONES:** **B**eard, **O**besity/Obstruction, **N**o teeth, **E**lderly, **S**leep apnea.
- See [Appendix B](#appendix-b-airway-assessment) for more detail.

#### Prediction of difficult laryngoscopy and tracheal intubation
- The mnemonic LEMON may help predict difficult laryngoscopy and tracheal intubation.
- **LEMON:** **L**ook externally (habitus, dentition, trauma), **E**valuate (3-3-2 rule), **M**allampati, **O**bstruction/Obesity, **N**eck mobility. 
- See [Appendix B](#appendix-b-airway-assessment) for more detail.


#### Recognition of physiologically difficult airway
- Induction of anesthesia, apnea, and positive pressure ventilation can cause major cardiorespiratory deterioration.
- Patients are at increased risk of peri-intubation decompensation when they have:
  - Hypotension or shock.
  - Severe hypoxemia.
  - Severe metabolic acidosis.
  - Right ventricular dysfunction or pulmonary hypertension.
  - Traumatic brain injury or raised intracranial pressure.


## Team roles
Skilled assistance is mandatory. Where possible, a team of 5 is required at the minimum.
- **Team leader:** the ICU SMO/SR or ICU nurse in charge.
- **Primary intubator:** who controls the airway.
- **Second intubator:** may be ICU SMO/SR.
- **Drug administration:** who may also co-ordinate the procedure.
- **Dedicated airway assistant:** who helps deliver airways/adjuncts to the primary intubator, and may be able to administer cricoid pressure or BURP if required.

Other important roles include:
- Runner
- Situational awareness (e.g. monitor vital signs, timekeeper)


⚠️ Note: **Cricoid pressure (CP)** should be *considered*  in all emergency situations and should be applied at the commencement of induction. CP may distort the larynx and make airway control difficult. If this is the case, remove the cricoid pressure and optimize the positioning to facilitate intubation of the trachea. CP is controversial.

## Drug selection
The patient's physiological state will determine the choice of intubation medications. Drug selection is guided by several key criteria:
- **Hemodynamic profile:**
  - Ketamine may preserve or elevate BP in shock.
  - Propofol may worsen hypotension.
- **Contraindications:** 
  - Avoid succinylcholine in burns, crush injuries, or neuromuscular disorders (including ICU-acquired weakness).
  - Avoid propofol in severe cardiac dysfunction.
- **Duration of paralysis:**
  - Short-acting succinylcholine for quick recovery vs rocuronium for prolonged cases.
- **Vasoactive support:**
  - Metaraminol, phenylephrine, or 'push-dose' adrenaline are strongly advised to prevent physiological deterioration. 


See [Appendix D](#appendix-d-drug-doses-for-induction-and-intubation) for drug doses.



## Failed intubation and difficult airway plan
**Plan** for difficult intubation, difficult ventilation, and failed intubation/ventilation. Discuss this as part of the airway management plan **prior to intubation**. 
Suggested plans are in [Appendix C](#appendix-c-management-of-difficult-intubation) from the Difficult Airway Society Guidelines 2025.

**High-risk intubations** e.g. oropharyngeal swelling, may require advanced techniques such as an awake fibre-optic intubation, and no more than three attempts at intubation should be made due to the higher risk of bleeding and swelling of the airway. Always consider anesthetic SMO support in these cases.

### Appendix D: Drug Doses for Induction and Intubation

#### Induction agents
| Medication | Dose (IV) | Onset Time | Duration | Key Considerations |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Midazolam** | 0.1–0.3 mg/kg | 60–90 s | 15–30 min | • Not usually recommended for RSI due to slow onset time |
| **Thiopental (thiopentone)** | 3–5 mg/kg | 30–45 s | 5–10 min | • Histamine release<br> • Myocardial depression, vasodilation, hypotension<br> • No analgesic properties |
| **Ketamine** | 1–2 mg/kg | 30–60 s | 5–10 min | • Cardiostimulatory properties<br> • Can increase airway secretions<br> • May cause hallucinations|
| **Etomidate** | 0.2–0.3 mg/kg | 30–60 s | 5–10 min | • Minimal cardiovascular effects<br> • No analgesic properties|
| **Propofol** | 1–2 mg/kg | <60 s | 5–10 min | • Rapid sedation<br> • Avoid in patients at risk for hypotension<br> • No analgesic properties |

#### Muscle relaxant

| Medication | Dose (IV) | Onset Time | Duration | Key Considerations |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Succinylcholine** | 1–1.5 mg/kg | 45–60 s | 6–10 min | • Fast onset<br> • Contraindicated in certain conditions (hyperK, MH, NMJ disease, burns, crush injury) |
| **Rocuronium** | 0.6–1.2 mg/kg | 1–2 min | >30 min | • Longer duration<br> • Preferred when succinylcholine is not suitable |


#### Adjuncts
These include drugs e.g. fentanyl which is often used as an induction adjunct. Other drugs listed here are related to hemodynamic support. **Onset and duration are guides**; tailor dose according to clinical need.

| Medication | Dose (IV) | Onset Time | Duration | Key Considerations |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Fentanyl** | 1–3 mcg/kg | 2–3 min | 30–60 min | • Blunts sympathetic response (tachycardia, hypertension)<br> • Omit or reduce in shock |
| **Atropine** | Adult: 0.5–1 mg<br>Peds: 0.02 mg/kg  | 1 min | 30–60 min | • Prevents or treats bradycardia (vagal response, succinylcholine, hypoxia)<br> • Consider in peds and before a 2nd succinylcholine dose |
| **Glycopyrrolate** | Adult: 0.2–0.4 mg <br>Peds: 4–10 mcg/kg | 1–2 min | 2–4 h | • Antisialagogue<br> • Dries secretions for awake/fiberoptic intubation<br> • Less tachycardia than atropine |
| **Metaraminol (push-dose)** | 0.5–1 mg every 2–5 min; infusion 1–10 mg/h | 1–2 min | 20–60 min | • Mainly α1 agonist (↑ SVR), some β1 and indirect NAdr release<br> • May cause reflex bradycardia<br> • Tachyphylaxis|
| **Phenylephrine (push-dose)** | 50–200 mcg every 2–5 min | <1 min | 10–20 min | • Pure α-1 agonist (↑ SVR)<br> • May cause reflex bradycardia |
| **Adrenaline (push-dose)** | 10–20 mcg every 2–5 min | 1–2 min | 2–10 min |  • α + β agonist (↑ BP and HR) |
